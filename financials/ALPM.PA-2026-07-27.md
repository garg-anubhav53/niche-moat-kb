## PART 1 — FIRST-PASS ANALYSIS

### §5.1 Moat Depth Analysis: How Deep Is the Process Weighing Moat?

**The core proposition**: Precia Molen sells weighing systems that are embedded in industrial production processes — belt weighers on bulk material conveyor lines, hopper/tank weighing cells under mixing vessels, checkweighers inline on food or pharma packaging lines, dosing and batching automation for chemical plants. These are not catalogue instruments delivered and forgotten; they are engineered, installed, calibrated, and maintained into a specific production flow.

**Source 1 — Regulatory certification lock-in (OIML / Legal-for-Trade)**: Many of Precia's systems operate as Legal-for-Trade instruments under OIML (International Organization of Legal Metrology) R50 and related standards. Once a system is installed and certified under OIML R50, the certification is issued for that specific equipment in that specific configuration at that specific plant location. Replacing a Precia belt weigher with a Schenck Process belt weigher does not transfer the certification — the customer must file for re-verification of the new equipment, pay for a third-party metrological audit, potentially halt the line during verification, and potentially re-qualify the process for pharmaceutical or food safety customers who have cited the specific equipment in their regulatory dossiers (e.g., FDA 21 CFR Part 11 environments, EMA GMP compliance). In chemical plants with ATEX (explosive atmosphere) certification, Precia holds EX-certifications for their equipment; switching vendors means obtaining new ATEX certification. The recertification cost alone can exceed the price delta of competing equipment by a factor of 2–5x. This is the hardest form of switching cost — it is not contractual but regulatory.

**Source 2 — Embedded software integration**: Precia's 2021 acquisition of CREATIVE IT (Lyon; €3.2M revenue; software for industrial process control) signals the company's strategic push into software-embedded moat. Once Precia's dosing/batching software is integrated into a customer's SCADA or DCS (Distributed Control System), the weighing hardware becomes a node in a larger software architecture. Ripping out the hardware means replacing or modifying the integration layer — expensive engineering work for the customer's automation team. This is analogous to the industrial automation moat that Rockwell Automation or Endress+Hauser exploit.

**Source 3 — Multi-year service contracts and calibration cycles**: Industrial weighing systems require periodic calibration (annually in most regulatory jurisdictions, quarterly for high-stakes dosing applications). Precia's service network — "maintenance, verification and technical support wherever weighing systems are installed" — generates recurring revenue from this mandatory maintenance cycle. A customer who has service contracts with Precia is unlikely to risk disruption by switching the underlying hardware vendor mid-contract. The service contracts also give Precia visibility into customer maintenance cycles and equipment age, enabling proactive replacement sales to the installed base. This is the classic aftermarket moat.

**Source 4 — Production line downtime cost asymmetry**: The switching cost calculation from a customer's perspective: if a checkweigher on a food production line goes down during a swap to a new vendor, an hour of downtime in a modern food packaging line costs €5,000–€20,000 in lost production. The price delta between Precia and a low-cost competitor on a checkweigher (~€20,000–€80,000 equipment cost) can be recovered in one production interruption. Customers rationally accept mild price premiums from incumbent suppliers to avoid transition risk.

**Competitive landscape and moat sustainability**:

*Mettler-Toledo (MTD: NYSE, ~$23B market cap):* Mettler-Toledo serves both laboratory and industrial segments. Their industrial process weighing (Mettler-Toledo Industrial Division) competes directly with Precia on checkweighers and belt weighers in food/pharma. MTD competes primarily at the premium end and is not a volume player in bulk/process weighing in Europe's chemical and mining segments where Precia is strongest. MTD's European channel is primarily direct sales in Germany and Switzerland, with less reach in France and Southern Europe's process industry base where Precia has 75 years of local presence. The competitive threat from MTD is real but is partially mitigated by: (a) MTD's focus on precision laboratory instruments where margins are higher; (b) Precia's local service network strength in Francophone markets; (c) Precia's ability to provide bespoke engineering solutions that MTD's more standardized product line cannot always match.

*Schenck Process (private; owned by Haver & Boecker):* The most direct belt weigher and bulk material weighing competitor. Schenck is German and has strong presence in heavy industry (steel, cement, mining). In continental European process industries, Schenck and Precia overlap most directly. However, Schenck has historically ceded ground in the French market to Precia (geographic home advantage) and in the food/pharma niche (Precia's EX and ATEX certifications and service network in France are well-established). No direct pricing collapse evidence found; both coexist with regional differentiation. Being private, Schenck's financials are not accessible to assess margin pressure.

*Minebea Intec (subsidiary of MinebeaMitsumi, Japan):* Specializes in checkweighers, metal detection, and high-precision inline weighing for food and pharma. A direct competitor in Precia's fastest-growing niche. Minebea Intec uses both strain-gauge and electromagnetic force compensation (EMF) technology. Minebea Intec's parent is a large Japanese MNC (~¥1T revenue) with capacity to price aggressively. This is perhaps the most credible competitive threat in Precia's food/pharma segment.

*Siemens Industrial Weighing (SIWAREX):* Siemens sells weighing modules that integrate into their S7 PLC/automation ecosystems. For customers already on Siemens automation, SIWAREX offers a deeply integrated path. However, Siemens is not a full process weighing solution provider — they sell components, not turnkey systems with local installation, calibration, and service. Precia competes with Siemens on the component level but has the advantage of providing integrated solutions including installation, commissioning, and ongoing maintenance.

**Moat verdict**: The process weighing moat is genuine but not impenetrable. It is best characterized as a **regional-incumbent network moat with regulatory enforcement** — customers who are already embedded in Precia's installed base face multi-year, multi-hundred-thousand euro switching costs when regulatory recertification, SCADA integration, and service network disruption are properly accounted for. New installations are competitive; replacements strongly favor incumbents. The moat strength varies by: (a) regulatory intensity of the end market (pharma > food > chemical > mining > logistics); (b) degree of software integration; (c) geography (strongest in France/Francophone Africa; contested in UK, Australia, emerging markets). This is a 7/10 moat — not as durable as Mettler-Toledo's laboratory moat but more durable than a generic capital equipment manufacturer.

### §5.2 Revenue Quality: Recurring vs. Capital Equipment, Cyclicality, Divestiture Impact

**Recurring vs. one-time revenue**: Precia Molen does not explicitly disclose a recurring/capex revenue split in accessible filings, which is the single biggest information gap. Based on first principles:

- *Service and calibration contracts*: French industrial regulations mandate annual calibration of Legal-for-Trade instruments. Precia's own materials state they deliver "maintenance, verification and technical support wherever weighing systems are installed." With ~700-800 employees and global service operations across 42 countries with 22 commercial subsidiaries, the service organization is substantial. Industry benchmarks for process instrumentation companies at Precia's scale suggest service/aftermarket revenue of 25–35% of total revenue is typical. If Precia achieves even 25% recurring (€42M), this creates a resilient base that limits downside cyclicality.
- *Capital equipment*: New installations, system integrations, and large replacement projects constitute the remaining 65–75%. This is the cyclical portion.

**COVID stress test as cyclicality proxy**: 2020 revenue was -0.1% total, -4.9% organic. In the context of a global pandemic that caused 15–30% revenue declines for typical industrial capex companies (see: Rockwell Automation -12% 2020; Endress+Hauser private but peer commentary suggests -8%), Precia's -4.9% organic decline is strikingly mild. This can only be explained by: (a) significant recurring service revenue cushioning the decline; (b) essential nature of food/pharma/chemical industry customers who did not pause capex; (c) Precia's calibration contracts being classified as essential maintenance. The COVID data is the strongest available evidence that this is not a pure-play cyclical capex business.

**2023–2024 industrial slowdown**: Revenue declined -0.8% in 2024 (€170.2M → €168.9M), recovering to +0.1% in FY2025. The 2023–2024 period coincided with European industrial manufacturing slowdown (PMI <50, weak German industrial orders). Precia's resilience relative to peers (many specialty industrial equipment companies saw -5% to -15% in this period) again supports the recurring service buffer thesis.

**Dibal divestiture — revenue base impact**:
The commercial weighing division was sold to Dibal Group in May 2025. The H1 2025 semi-annual report shows the divestiture impacted H1 2025 revenue by only -€0.6M, suggesting the commercial weighing activities by May 2025 had already been wound down or ring-fenced (the preliminary agreement was announced March 2025, finalization May 2025). The total commercial weighing revenue in FY2024 is estimated from the §3.5 baseline at roughly €25–35M (the pro-forma process-only base is estimated at €130–145M against FY2025 total of €169M). The exact revenue of the divested unit is not confirmed by accessible filings.

**Key implication**: FY2026 will report purely process/industrial revenue on a full-year basis. This means reported FY2026 revenue will likely be lower than €169M in absolute terms (-€15–€30M for the divested division). However: (a) if the commercial weighing division was margin-dilutive (which appears likely, as commercial weighing is a more competitive, lower-margin business), EBIT margins should improve; (b) organic growth of the process business at 2–4% annually on the cleaned-up base (€130–145M) puts FY2026 process revenue at approximately €133–150M. The FY2026 headline revenue will look like a "decline" but will be a mix-improvement story.

### FM-01: Moat Illusion — "Process Weighing Moat" is Regulatory Theater, Not Real Switching Cost

**The attack**: OIML certification and ATEX certification requirements sound impressive, but re-certification is not prohibitively expensive for a large industrial customer. A chemical plant spending €50M+ on a capex cycle does not blink at a €30,000 re-verification fee. The real switching cost is just inertia. Mettler-Toledo or Minebea Intec can offer a 10–15% price discount and customers will switch. Precia's pricing power is an illusion because competition has increased with globalization.

**Counter-evidence**: The COVID resilience data (-4.9% organic in 2020 vs. typical -10–20% for industrial capex peers) strongly suggests customers did not switch or reduce maintenance contracts even during a severe shock. If switching costs were low, maintenance contract cancellations would have been the first customer response to budget pressure. Furthermore, Precia's 75-year operating history and consistent 7–10% EBIT margins over decades (not just recent years) would not be sustainable in a structurally unmoated business — margin compression would have appeared by now.

**More nuanced view**: The moat is REAL but ASYMMETRIC. It protects the installed base (replacements heavily favor Precia) but does NOT protect new installation wins where competitive bidding is fierce. Precia must earn new customers through price/performance competition; it only exploits the regulatory moat on renewals and maintenance. This means revenue growth is harder to achieve than one might assume from the moat description — it requires winning new projects competitively, not just defending existing relationships.

**Severity: MED** (moat is real on installed base but does not guarantee growth)
**Probability: MED** (some pricing pressure on new project wins is plausible; installed base moat is solid)
**Red-team verdict: PARTIALLY VALID — moat protects the floor but does not guarantee the ceiling**

### FM-02: Cyclicality Trap — Industrial Capex Collapses in the Next Downturn

**The attack**: Industrial capex is deeply cyclical. In a 2026–2028 European recession scenario (ECB overtightening, German manufacturing recession extends, construction sector weakness), chemical and food processing companies freeze capex. Precia's new-equipment revenue (assumed 65–75% of total) collapses 20–30%. Even if service holds, total revenue could fall to €100–120M and EBIT to €5–8M. At €135.7M market cap, this is a P/E of 17–27x — dangerously stretched for a business in trough earnings.

**Counter-evidence**: The 2020 COVID data is the most rigorous cyclicality test available. In the worst demand shock in a generation, Precia's organic revenue declined only -4.9%. Two explanations: (a) the service/maintenance recurring base is large enough to cushion the cycle; (b) food, pharma, and chemical customers are essential-industry capex spenders who maintained investment through COVID. The 2023–2024 European industrial slowdown (PMI sub-50) produced only a -0.8% revenue decline in FY2024 — again dramatically outperforming industrial peers.

**The genuine risk**: A prolonged European deindustrialization scenario (steel, chemicals relocating outside EU due to energy costs and regulation) would permanently shrink Precia's addressable market in its core geographies. This is a structural shift, not a cyclical dip, and the COVID analogy provides no defense against it.

**Severity: MED** (demonstrated resilience limits downside, but structural European industrial decline is a real tail risk)
**Probability: LOW-MED** (cyclical risk is real but limited; structural deindustrialization is a tail risk but growing in probability given EU industrial policy environment)
**Red-team verdict: REAL BUT MANAGEABLE — existing data suggests ~5% revenue floor in downturns; structural risk requires monitoring**

### FM-03: Technology Obsolescence — AI/Vision-Based Weighing Makes Strain Gauges Obsolete

**The attack**: Computer vision and AI can now estimate package weight in logistics with sufficient accuracy for many applications (Amazon's Just Walk Out stores use vision-based item tracking without physical scales). IoT-connected pressure/flow sensors are replacing dedicated weighing instruments in chemical processes. Strain-gauge and load-cell technology (Precia's core) is 70-year-old physics. Within 5–10 years, the addressable market for dedicated weighing hardware could shrink dramatically as software-based estimation supplants hardware measurement.

**Counter-evidence**: This threat is real in consumer/retail contexts (Amazon cashierless checkout, self-checkout scales) but faces fundamental physics barriers in process/industrial applications. A cement plant's belt weigher operating at 5,000 tonnes/hour in a dusty, vibrating environment at temperatures of -20°C to +60°C requires physical force measurement. Vision-based estimation cannot achieve the ±0.25% accuracy required for Legal-for-Trade bulk measurement in these conditions. Pharma dosing applications require certified load cell measurement to GMP standards — AI estimation is not acceptable for regulatory compliance. The most vulnerable segment is logistics (parcel checkweighing where dimensional weighing is emerging), but this is not Precia's primary market.

**More nuanced risk**: Digital/IoT transformation could erode the *hardware* component of Precia's revenue even if the underlying need persists. Customers might buy fewer dedicated weighing instruments but demand more software, connectivity, and data analytics. This is a margin mix shift rather than an obsolescence — Precia's CREATIVE IT acquisition is a direct hedge. However, if the company is too slow in the software transition, digital-first competitors could redefine the competitive battlefield.

**Severity: LOW-MED** (hardware is defensible in industrial process applications; software transition risk is real)
**Probability: LOW** (5–10 year timeline; Precia's software push (CREATIVE IT) provides a partial hedge; physics constraints protect the core industrial market)
**Red-team verdict: MONITOR, NOT DISMISS — the software integration strategy matters more than the hardware threat**

### FM-07: Family Governance / Minority Protection Risk

**The attack**: Escharavil family holds ~43.5% of capital but 59.1% of voting rights — this implies dual-class or super-voting shares. On Euronext Growth Paris (not the regulated Euronext main market), disclosure and governance requirements are lighter. A family with 59.1% voting control can: (a) approve related-party transactions at non-arm's-length pricing; (b) block a premium acquisition that minorities would benefit from; (c) deploy the €40–44M net cash in a way that benefits family-related entities; (d) set executive compensation without effective minority check.

**The specific concern**: The Supervisory Board is chaired by Anne-Marie Escharavil, with Luc and Alice and Ingrid Escharavil as members. This is not a governance structure with robust independent oversight. Independent directors exist but cannot outvote family members plus family-aligned institutional holders on contentious decisions.

**Counter-evidence**: Precia has paid dividends consistently (€0.55/share proposed for FY2025), maintained B1+ credit, and has operated transparently for 4+ generations under family stewardship. The small acquisitions (CREATIVE IT, Scaletec, Reunion) have been bolt-ons at modest prices — not empire-building. The sale of the commercial weighing division to Dibal appears to be a value-accretive strategic decision, not a related-party extraction. The B1+ Banque de France credit rating requires a credible governance track record.

**Structural risk that is real but bounded**: The family's 59.1% voting control means a hostile takeover cannot occur without family consent, which limits minority's upside from M&A premium. However, the same control prevents a value-destroying hostile acquisition OF Precia. Net effect: minority investors are protected from downside (family won't sell to a hostile buyer at a fire-sale price) but also capped on upside (no takeover premium unless family agrees). This is governance risk, not governance failure.

**Severity: MED** (voting control limits exit via M&A premium; related-party risk structurally elevated)
**Probability: LOW** (multi-generational track record is clean; no evidence of minority abuse; dividend consistent)
**Red-team verdict: REAL STRUCTURAL CONSTRAINT ON UPSIDE, NOT ACUTE RISK — price in a 5–10% governance discount; it is already in the multiple**

### FM-08: French Regulatory / Labor Cost Risk — Restructuring Is Prohibitively Expensive

**The attack**: France's labor laws (Code du Travail) impose significant restructuring costs. If process weighing revenue declines 10–15% in a downturn, laying off even 100 of 700–800 employees would cost €3–5M in redundancy and social obligations, and take 18–24 months through mandatory consultation (CSE — Comité Social et Économique). This means Precia cannot rapidly reduce costs in a downturn, and operating leverage works negatively — revenue declines flow almost directly to EBIT.

**Quantification**: If revenue falls 10% (€169M → €152M) and the variable cost base (materials, some labor) can only be reduced by 5% (the rest is fixed/quasi-fixed), EBIT falls from €17.1M to approximately €8–10M. This halves earnings per share and re-prices the stock accordingly.

**Counter-evidence**: Three observations: (a) Precia's 2020 data shows only -4.9% organic revenue decline in a severe global shock, suggesting demand is more resilient than typical industrial peers; (b) Precia has maintained employment throughout its history without evidence of large restructuring charges, suggesting management calibrates hiring conservatively; (c) the service revenue base (recurring maintenance contracts) provides a floor that sustains employment more naturally than pure project-based businesses.

**Genuine concern**: The EX-certification requirements for staff (Precia's technicians performing work in ATEX zones need specific certifications) mean the workforce is specialized and difficult to quickly flex down or hire back up. This creates a specific hysteresis risk — if Precia is too slow to hire during an upswing, they miss revenue; too slow to reduce during a downswing, margins compress.

**Severity: MED** (French labor rigidity limits downside speed of cost adjustment)
**Probability: MED** (French labor cost rigidity is structural and real; offset by business resilience)
**Red-team verdict: REAL BUT PARTIALLY MITIGATED — operating leverage is the true variable, and Precia's history suggests cost management has been reasonable; monitor labor cost/revenue ratio**

### FM-09: Dibal Divestiture Quality — Was Commercial Weighing Profitable or a Drag?

**The attack**: If the commercial weighing division was profitable (say, 8–10% EBIT margin), selling it removed €2.5–3.5M of EBIT from the group. FY2025 EBIT of €17.1M was reported WITH the commercial division still in place through May 2025 (only 5 months of the year). The H1 2025 and FY2025 EBIT therefore include commercial weighing profit. FY2026 process-only EBIT could be €14–15M if commercial weighing was a meaningful profit contributor.

**The alternative (and more likely) scenario**: Commercial weighing (retail scales for butchers, supermarkets, etc.) is a structurally lower-margin, commoditized business. Dibal Group, a Spanish specialist in commercial scales, is the logical acquirer for exactly this reason — they have the scale and channel to make retail weighing profitable that Precia does not. It is more likely that commercial weighing was margin-dilutive (below-group EBIT margin) and the divestiture is margin-accretive for the remaining process business.

**Evidence supporting divestiture as quality-improvement**: Management commentary on "intensifying operational performance" and focusing on "key strategic" areas post-divestiture aligns with exiting a drag business. The fact that the preliminary agreement was announced just 5 months before completion (March → May 2025) suggests the strategic review was not prolonged — management knew it was a non-core drag.

**Residual uncertainty**: Without the purchase price paid by Dibal and the segment EBIT disclosed in annual filings (accessible only in French-language documents), we cannot confirm the margin profile of the divested division. This is a genuine data gap.

**Severity: MED** (if commercial was profitable, FY2026 EBIT could disappoint vs. FY2025)
**Probability: LOW-MED** (weight of evidence suggests commercial was margin-dilutive; divestiture is likely quality-improving)
**Red-team verdict: MATERIAL UNCERTAINTY BUT PROBABLY UPSIDE — the divestiture is more likely to improve than harm EBIT; requires FY2026 first results to confirm**

### FM-10: Net Cash Sustainability — Does Precia Actually Need the €44M for Operations?

**The attack**: The €44M net cash figure sounds impressive relative to the €135.7M cap, but: (a) French industrial businesses carry large working capital (WC) — receivables from industrial project invoicing can stretch 60–90 days; payables to suppliers are typically 45–60 days; net WC can absorb €15–20M of apparent cash; (b) the B/S "net cash" as reported may include cash reserved for ongoing project commitments (contractual guarantees, performance bonds); (c) capex for manufacturing and service equipment is ongoing; (d) Precia's global operations in 42 countries require operational cash reserves in multiple jurisdictions, some of which may not be repatriable to the French parent at low cost.

**Quantification of genuine excess cash**: If we assume: operational cash requirements of €10–15M (WC buffer, capex maintenance, guaranteed bonds), the true distributable/investable excess cash is closer to €25–35M — still material at 18–26% of market cap, but less than the headline 32%.

**Free cash flow quality**: If EBIT is €17.1M and net income is €11.0M, the gap (€6.1M) represents: D&A (positive FCF), interest income (positive given net cash), taxes, and other. Net income appears to understate FCF (D&A not disclosed from accessible sources but typically 2–3% of revenue = €3–5M), suggesting FCF of €14–18M annually before capex. With maintenance capex of €4–6M (industrial manufacturer's typical 2.5–3.5% of revenue), FCF is approximately €8–14M/year. At this rate, the net cash position grows by €5–11M per year (after dividend of ~€3M). The cash is real and growing.

**Severity: LOW** (net cash is real; operating requirements are modest given size; FCF generation is solid)
**Probability: LOW** (cash sustainability is NOT a risk; the concern is the opposite — cash is idle)
**Red-team verdict: MOSTLY DISMISSED — the risk is cash not being deployed productively (FM-11), not cash being needed for operations**

**The attack**: With €40–48M net cash and a €135.7M market cap, Precia's management is in the enviable but dangerous position of having 30%+ of market cap in free cash. The temptation to "do something" with it — a transformative acquisition, an international expansion into an unfamiliar market, an unrelated diversification — is structurally dangerous for a family-controlled micro-cap. One bad €20M acquisition at 2x revenue in a slow-growth adjacent market could reduce EBIT margins to 6–7% and eliminate the net cash advantage, repricing the stock to €15–18.

**Track record analysis** (what we know):

If Precia remains micro-cap and under-covered by 2028 with €55–65M in net cash, the pressure to do something increases. The risk is not today's management team but the board decision under future circumstances.

**Severity: MED** (a bad acquisition of €20–30M would be material to the investment thesis)
**Probability: LOW-MED** (track record is disciplined; family governance limits empire-building; but structural pressure grows with cash accumulation)

### FM-12: Accounting / Income Statement Opacity — Is the 10.1% EBIT Real?

**The attack**: Precia uses a cost-by-nature income statement, which means: revenue → other operating income → raw materials and consumables → external services → payroll → D&A → other operating expenses = EBIT. The "gross margin" aggregator (~78–79%) is meaningless because it does not include payroll or other production costs. This format, while permitted under IFRS, is less transparent than a cost-by-function P&L (revenue → COGS → gross profit → SGA → EBIT). Specifically, a cost-by-nature format:
- Could obscure capitalized development costs flowing through the balance sheet rather than the P&L (artificially inflating EBIT)
- Makes it difficult to verify that inventory is not being built to absorb fixed costs (classic factory utilization trick)
- External services line could include related-party transactions at non-arm's-length
- D&A policies are not immediately transparent to web-based research

**Are there red flags?**: The net income/EBIT relationship (€11.0M net income / €17.1M EBIT = 64% conversion) implies approximately €6.1M of below-EBIT items. These would typically be: interest income (positive, given net cash — say +€1–2M), taxes (corporate tax in France ~25% = -€4–5M on pre-tax income), minority interests (small), and finance costs on leases (IFRS 16 capitalized leases appear as finance cost). This arithmetic is consistent — no obvious red flag in the NI/EBIT bridge.

**Capex vs. D&A**: We do not have confirmed capex/D&A data from accessible web sources. If D&A >> capex (i.e., amortization of acquired intangibles inflates D&A, making FCF higher than EBIT but also meaning EBIT is lower than "cash earnings"), this is actually positive for FCF quality. If capex >> D&A (i.e., the company is under-investing relative to depreciation), this would inflate EBIT short-term but create future capex catch-up. The track record of stable margins for decades argues against the latter.

**One-time items**: The FY2025 "record" 10.1% EBIT margin requires scrutiny — is it truly record, or were there one-time items (gain on Dibal divestiture, deferred restructuring charges)? The EQS press release describes EBIT/ROC at 10.1% as record, but accessible web research does not confirm whether the Dibal divestiture generated a book gain included in EBIT or taken below the line.

**Severity: MED** (opacity is real; specific Dibal one-time gain question is material)
**Probability: LOW** (decades of consistent margins and B1+ credit rating argue against material accounting manipulation; the NI/EBIT bridge is arithmetically consistent)
**Red-team verdict: OPACITY IS REAL; MANIPULATION RISK IS LOW — seek to clarify Dibal divestiture accounting treatment in the FY2025 annual report**
